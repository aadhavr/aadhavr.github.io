Do exam leaks push young Indian men toward the manosphere?

A stacked event study of nine exam leaks, with court records, Google Trends, and YouTube comments data

political economy
Author

Aadhav Rajesh

Published

October 10, 2026

A few weeks ago, I attended a talk organized by Georgetown’s South Asian Society, where I had the pleasure of listening to Professor Tariq Omar Ali and Professor Irfan Nooruddin discuss the recent protests in India1, and more broadly the wave of protests across the region over the last few years. One of the interesting areas of discussion was what the “maleness” of these protests was like, and I was curious to see if there was a measurable spillover of the frustration from young men in South Asia struggling to go to college and get jobs onto online spaces and the manosphere.

To see what this looks like, take the Uttarakhand graduate-level exam of September 2025, which was cancelled after its paper leaked. Press play and watch how districts light up after the leak, and mostly inside the state that ran the exam. You can switch to any of the other leaks from the menu.

Government jobs in India are scarce and are handed out through competitive exams.2 When an exam leaks, I expected that young men who lose out take their frustration to a protest, and from there it spills into resentment of women (e.g., of women’s quotas, women’s jobs, feminism, etc.). I tested this hypothesis by using protest records for every district in India from 2023 to 20263, court cases, surveys, search data and online comments. Here’s what I found:

  1. Exam leaks put people protesting on the street quickly, and only in the state that ran the exam (non-party exam protests: \(\hat\beta = 0.00045\), randomisation \(p = 0.048\); Section 1).
  2. Protests appear to be predominantly male, but that may come from how protests are recorded and police intervention at protests which may deter some women from attending (exam protests meet intervention or force in 12.9% of cases, against 5.2% of other protests; descriptive).
  3. I find no sign of the spillover. Most comments blame no one in particular and, of those that do, about nine in ten blame the exam body or the government (of 28,121 comments: exam body 18.4%, government 14.2%, no one in particular 63.2%, women or women’s quotas 0.31%).

I’ll break down how I arrived at these conclusions.

1. Exam leaks put people protesting on the street

I followed nine exam leaks between 2023 and 2026, from Gujarat’s junior clerk exam to the UP police constable exam that 48 lakh people applied for4. In the state that ran each exam, protests about exams jump in the first two weeks after the exam date (\(\hat\beta = 0.00045\) protests per district-week per applicant per 1,000 youth; cluster \(p = 0.018\), randomisation \(p = 0.048\); 9 shocks, 640 districts, weeks \(-8\) to \(+8\)). Elsewhere in India, nothing changes.5

The protest data come from ACLED, the Armed Conflict Location & Event Data project, which records every reported protest in India from news sources, with a date, a district and a short description written by its coders.6 I keep the protests about a specific recruitment, entrance or licensing exam: a keyword filter first, then a language model applied a fixed written rule, checked against hand labels.7 That leaves 2,450 exam protests in 640 districts (Census 2011 boundaries), from January 2023 to September 2026.

The nine leaks are the shocks.8 For each one I measure how exposed each district is: \(A_{de}\), the number of applicants to exam \(e\) per 1,000 residents of district \(d\) aged 15-29,9 which is zero outside the state that ran the exam. I then compare districts with more and less exposure, week by week, around the exam date, in a stacked event study:

\[ y_{dt} = \sum_{k \neq -1} \beta_k \, A_{de} \, \mathbf{1}[t - T_e = k] + \alpha_{de} + \gamma_{te} + \varepsilon_{dt} \]

Here \(y_{dt}\) is the number of exam protests in district \(d\) in week \(t\), \(T_e\) is the week of exam \(e\), \(\alpha_{de}\) are district-by-shock fixed effects (each district’s usual level of protest around each exam) and \(\gamma_{te}\) are week-by-shock fixed effects (anything that hits all of India in that week, such as national news). Each \(\beta_k\) is the extra protest in week \(k\) for each extra applicant per 1,000 young people, relative to week \(-1\); a control for exposure to other shocks in their own post-period is included. Week 0 is the exam date, which is fixed months in advance, not the cancellation date, which protest itself can bring forward.10 The headline estimate is the mean of \(\beta_k\) after the exam minus the mean before it.

The identifying assumption is that, without the leak, exam protest in high- and low-exposure districts would have moved in parallel. The weeks before the exam are the check, and they are flat (linear pre-trend slope, \(p = 0.16\)).

Treatment varies across only six exposed states and one national exam, so standard errors clustered by state are unreliable. The p-values I lean on come from randomisation inference instead: I move all nine exam dates together by every eligible number of weeks (164 shifts), re-run the whole analysis each time, and ask how often a fake set of dates gives an effect at least as large as the real one.11

The clearest case is Uttarakhand in 2025. In the week after the leak, exam protests went from almost none to about 13 per 10 million young people12, while the rest of the country stayed flat. For the UP constable leak, the average effect means roughly 16 extra protests across the state in the two months after the exam (1.77 a week, 95% CI 0.22 to 3.33, against a baseline of 0.25 a week)13, in a state that normally sees almost none.

NEET-UG is a national exam, so no single state is exposed. What varies is how many candidates each state has per young person, and that is the variation the design uses for it.14

So who is on the street? As you may expect, there are political parties organizing, but not as many as you’d think. About 40% of exam protests in the data are organised by a party or its youth wing (1,035 of 2,450, or 42%)15, but their protests don’t rise more in the places where more people took the exam that leaked (party-led: \(\hat\beta = 0.00014\), cluster \(p = 0.11\), randomisation \(p = 0.27\); non-party: \(\hat\beta = 0.00045\), cluster \(p = 0.018\), randomisation \(p = 0.048\)).

The protests that do follow the candidates are the rest, the ones led by aspirants, students and their unions. That holds even if I count the student unions with party links as party protests (non-party, broad definition: \(\hat\beta = 0.00041\), randomisation \(p = 0.048\)). The data can’t confirm that each protester sat the exam, but the response to a leak comes from where the candidates live, and from groups that describe themselves as students and aspirants.16

2. Protests appear to be predominantly male, but not only because fewer women protest

In the 2023 World Values Survey, 11% of Indian women said they had ever attended a demonstration, against 27% of men (men 27.0%, women 11.1%, weighted; a gap of 15.0 points, robust \(p < 0.001\), \(n = 1{,}197\); among 18-29-year-olds, men 24.8% and women 8.9%)17. Self-reports about protest of any kind are only a rough guide to who turns up at exam protests, but they suggest women are a minority there, perhaps around three in ten.18

ACLED’s descriptions are short summaries its coders write from news reports. I search them with fixed word lists: a report mentions women if it uses “women,” “woman,” “female,” “girl(s),” “mahila” or “ladies”; it is sex-neutral if it describes people only with words such as “students,” “aspirants,” “candidates” or “youth” and uses no gendered word.19

The record, though, seems to go further than the street. Only 2% of ACLED reports on exam protests mention women at all, against 11% of ACLED reports on all protests (2.1% against 11.0% of all 86,500 events; 95% intervals in the figure below), and the gap holds within the same news outlets (exam minus other reports from the same source: \(-8.4\) points, 95% CI \(-9.9\) to \(-7.4\); 32 sources, 2,516 exam reports)20. When a report does say who protested, 96% of the time it uses a word with no sex: “students,” “aspirants” or “candidates” (96.3%, 95% CI 95.3 to 97.2, of the 1,526 exam reports that name any kind of person; 44.2% for other events).21 It’s worth noting that these reports are coders’ summaries of the news, so they tell us about the record, not directly about the crowd.

Court records are one place where people have to be named, so I looked at who files exam-related cases. About a third of petitioners in exam-related court cases are women (33% of first petitioners whose sex can be identified, among 6,821 orders matching exam terms)22, close to their 32% share of the UP constable applicant pool23. Petitioners aren’t protesters, so this doesn’t tell us who is on the street, but it fits a picture in which women are present while less visible than men.

I also wanted to note that exam protests meet police intervention or force more than twice as often as other protests (13% against 5%; 12.9%, 95% CI 11.7 to 14.4, of 2,356 exam protests, against 5.2%, 5.0 to 5.3, of 74,666 other protests)24. That doesn’t explain the gap in the record, but it may keep some women away from exam protests in the first place, which would make the crowds themselves more male.

3. No sign of male backlash online

Google Trends gives a relative search index by state and week. I pulled 19 terms in five groups, each in the same request as a fixed anchor term (“weather”) so that they share a scale, and ran the same event-study design at the state level on log(term/anchor), for 2023 to 2025.25 A null result only means something if the test could have found an effect, so the design includes a positive control, searches about the leak itself, and reports a minimum detectable effect: the 95th percentile of the estimates from fake exam dates.

If frustrated young men were turning their anger on women, a leak should leave some trace online. After a leak, searches for “paper leak” rise by about 5% in the affected state (+4.7%, randomisation \(p = 0.018\); smallest detectable change 1.5%), so the method can see a local reaction. Searches for manosphere, men’s-rights and women’s-quota terms barely move. None rises by more than 1%, and men’s-rights searches actually dip slightly (manosphere: +0.9%, randomisation \(p = 0.31\), smallest detectable change 2.8%; women and jobs: +0.3%, \(p = 0.20\), smallest detectable 0.6%; men’s rights: \(-0.2\%\), \(p = 0.018\); all groups combined: +0.4%, \(p = 0.25\)).26 For the manosphere terms, the test could have picked up a rise of about 3%, so I can rule out a large spillover, but not a small one.

I collected comments with the YouTube Data API from videos returned by searches about each leak and the protests after it, within each shock window, and a language model (Claude Haiku) labelled whom each comment blames and whether it blames women or uses manosphere framing.27

In 28,000 YouTube comments on videos about leaks and protests, blame goes to exam bodies (18%) and governments (14%; of the 28,121 comments, 63.2% blame no one in particular). Fewer than one comment in 300 blames women (0.31%, an upper bound; the share of comments with gendered blame does not rise after a leak: \(-0.3\) points, randomisation \(p = 0.62\)).28

And the attitude at the heart of the backlash story, that men should come first when jobs are scarce, is not a young man’s grievance. In Pew’s 2019-20 India survey, 83% of men and 81% of women agreed with it, and young men were no more likely to agree than older men (weighted, \(n = 28{,}868\); women agree 2.3 points less than men, robust \(p = 0.002\); young adults are no more likely to agree, \(p = 0.14\); the gender gap among the young is no larger, \(p = 0.14\))29.

Conclusions

The young men in the photographs are real, but the photographs may exaggerate them. Exam leaks consistently put aspirants on the street. Women appear to be a minority of that street, and the record then seems to turn the whole crowd into “aspirants,” which many readers may hear as male30. What I could not find is the spillover I set out to test. If there is a gender story in India’s exam crisis, it may be less about male rage than about whose frustration gets seen.

Exam protests meet intervention or force more than twice as often as other protests, which may raise the cost of protest most for those already least present, including women. Ultimately, I find no evidence that leaks feed gendered online resentment. And the belief that men should come first for jobs is not a young man’s grievance: in Pew’s survey, women hold it almost as often as men (83% and 81%). The World Values Survey shows a wider gap (64% and 52%), but in both surveys about half or more of women agree, and young men are no more likely to agree than older men.

A2. Design and identifying assumptions

For each shock \(e\), all 640 districts \(d\) are observed for event weeks \(k = -8\) to \(+8\) around the exam week. Exposure \(A_{de}\) is applicants per 1,000 residents aged 15-29 (Census 2011), spread within the state by youth population and set to zero outside the exposed state.

\[ y_{dt} = \sum_{k \neq -1} \beta_k \, A_{de} \, \mathbf{1}[t - T_e = k] + \alpha_{de} + \gamma_{te} + \varepsilon_{dt} \]

\(\alpha_{de}\) are district-by-shock fixed effects and \(\gamma_{te}\) week-by-shock fixed effects; a control for exposure to other shocks in their post-period is included. Coefficients are re-centred on the pre-period mean; the headline is mean(post) minus mean(pre).

Absent the leak, exam protest in high- and low-exposure districts would have moved together. Supported by flat pre-trends (linear pre-slope \(p = 0.16\)) and by the pre-period-only trend check. Week 0 is the exam date, fixed months in advance. The cancellation date is endogenous: with it as week 0, the peak falls in weeks -1 and 0, consistent with protest causing cancellation. I switched to the exam date after seeing this pattern. Protest by aspirants from exposed states in other states (e.g. Delhi) would bias the estimate toward zero. Exposure varies only across states, and within states by imputed youth population, so the estimate is best read as an average effect of exposure intensity across shocks, which requires stronger assumptions than a binary DiD.

A3. Inference

Treatment varies across six exposed states plus one national shock, so CRV1 standard errors are reported only as indicative. The headline p-value is randomisation inference: all shock dates are shifted together by every eligible number of weeks (164 shifts on 2023-2026 data), preserving the spacing and overlap between shocks, and p is the share of shifts with an estimate at least as large in absolute value (minimum attainable about 0.006). An earlier version that drew independent false dates per shock produced false positives on simulated null data and was replaced. The test is conservative when false windows land on real, unlisted protest waves.

A6. Measurement

I measure exam protests with a keyword prefilter on ACLED event descriptions, then Claude Haiku applies a fixed written rule (a specific recruitment, entrance or licensing exam, or a specific recruitment process; excluding general unemployment, recruitment policy, demands by current employees, job-compensation promises, and university or school exams). Rule refined on 200 hand-labelled events (agreement 0.99, κ = 0.975, optimistic); on 50 fresh events, 92% agreement with an independent, locally run model (κ = 0.83) and 48 of 50 correct after hand resolution.

For party-led protests, ACLED actor fields are matched against a fixed list of parties and adult youth wings (1,035 of 2,450 exam protests).

To find mentions of women or sex-neutral wording, I use regular expressions on ACLED’s coder-written summaries, not original articles.

A7. Deviations, multiple testing and primary tests

Three choices were made after seeing data: the exam date as week 0 (see A2), the calendar-shift randomisation (see A3), and the party split, introduced when extending the data to 2026 weakened the combined estimate; the largest false-date estimates traced to a July 2026 wave of Samajwadi Party and Congress protests in Uttar Pradesh. All versions are reported. Primary tests: the non-party event study, the Google Trends test with its positive control, and the survey protest gap. Everything else is secondary or exploratory; with this many tests, some p-values near 0.05 should be expected by chance.

A8. Methods for parts 2 and 3

For court data, I use the Indian High Court and Supreme Court judgments metadata (Dattam Labs, AWS Open Data Registry, CC BY 4.0); 6,821 orders matching exam terms, dated by filing; petitioner sex by LLM (95.5% agreement with a second model; precision 0.97 and recall 1.00 against a third); state-level event study with a 26-week window (RI \(p = 0.013\)); driven mainly by UP constable (without it, RI \(p = 0.17\)).

For Google Trends, I use 19 terms in five groups, each request including the anchor term “weather”; index = mean log(term/anchor) by state-week; state-level version of the same design; minimum detectable effect = 95th percentile of |false-date estimates|. Hindi-script terms were added after the first null and returned no data. Covers 2023-2025.

For YouTube comments, I call YouTube Data API; 28,121 comments in shock windows labelled by LLM for gendered blame, manosphere framing and blame target; labels validated only on a small sample. Timing test: gendered blame -0.3 points (RI \(p = 0.62\)); manosphere framing +0.07 points from a 0.05% base (RI \(p = 0.044\)), treated as exploratory.

I also use WVS Wave 7 India (2023, n = 1,692; protest item Q209, jobs item Q33 on a 5-point scale) and the Pew Research Center India survey (2019-20, n = 29,999; jobs item on a 4-point scale; no employment variable in the public file). I weighted linear probability models with region or state fixed effects and robust errors (few clusters).

For Bangladesh and Nepal, I also use ACLED; single dated shocks (5 June 2024; 8 September 2025) compared with all other windows; descriptive.

A9. Data, credits and reproducibility

ACLED (acleddata.com) event data are not redistributed; figures show aggregates. District boundaries: DataMeet. Population: Census of India 2011. Large language models were used to classify text, as described in A6 and A8. Code is available in the repository linked at the top; data access instructions are in its README.
